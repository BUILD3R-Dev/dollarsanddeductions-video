> Ask ten business owners how their LLC is taxed, and you'll hear a lot of confident answers that aren't quite right. Here's the surprise. As far as the IRS is concerned, there's no such thing as LLC taxes. An LLC is a legal structure. How it's taxed is a separate choice, and there are four possible answers. Which one applies to you changes your forms, your deadlines, and how much you pay.

### 03 · State law vs the IRS  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Two different systems / The state creates it. / The IRS decides how it's taxed.

> Start here. You form an LLC under state law. It's about ownership and liability, and it can limit your personal exposure to the business's debts and lawsuits. But federal tax law doesn't have an LLC category. Instead, the IRS gives every LLC a default tax treatment, based on how many owners it has, and lets the LLC elect something different.
<sub>343 chars</sub>

### 04 · Four ways to be taxed  `BulletBuild`
*On screen:* How an LLC can be taxed / Four possibilities / ☑ Disregarded entity / ☑ Partnership / ☑ S corporation / ☑ C corporation

> So an LLC can be taxed four ways. With one owner, the default is a disregarded entity. With two or more owners, the default is a partnership. Either kind can elect to be taxed as an S corporation, by filing Form twenty-five fifty-three. Or as a C corporation, by filing Form eighty-eight thirty-two. Let's take them one at a time.

### 05 · The single-member default  `BulletBuild`
*On screen:* One owner, by default / Disregarded entity / ☑ Profit goes on Schedule C / ☑ Income tax at your rates / ☑ Self-employment tax / ☑ QBI deduction may apply

> With one owner, disregarded means the IRS looks right through the LLC for income tax. The profit goes on Schedule C, as part of your personal return, exactly like a sole proprietor. You pay income tax at your own rates. You pay self-employment tax, fifteen point three percent, on ninety-two point three five percent of the profit. And you may get the QBI deduction we covered in episode five.

### 07 · Taxed on profit  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Taxed on profit, / not on what you withdraw.

> Here's a mistake that catches a lot of people. A pass-through is taxed on its profit, whether or not you take the money out. If your LLC earns eighty thousand, and you leave forty thousand in the business account, you're still taxed on the full eighty. Money you leave in the business doesn't lower your tax.
<sub>308 chars</sub>

### 08 · Owner draws  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Owner draws / aren't a salary.

> The flip side: what you take out isn't an expense. In a default LLC, you pay yourself with owner draws, simple transfers from the business account to your personal one. Draws aren't deductible, and they aren't taxed separately, because the profit was already taxed. And a single-member LLC owner can't put themselves on payroll as a W-2 employee under the default treatment.
<sub>374 chars</sub>

### 09 · The partnership default  `BulletBuild`
*On screen:* Two or more owners / Partnership / ☑ Form 1065 / ☑ A K-1 for each member / ☑ Members pay the tax / ☑ Guaranteed payments

> With two or more owners, the default is partnership taxation. The LLC files Form ten sixty-five, an information return that's due March fifteenth for calendar-year businesses. It sends each member a Schedule K-1 showing their share of the profit, and each member pays tax on that share on their own return. Members who work in the business can also receive guaranteed payments for their services.
<sub>396 chars</sub>

### 10 · The return pays no tax  `KineticType`
*On screen:* The partnership return / pays no tax.

> The partnership return itself pays no federal income tax. But don't let that make it feel optional. Filing it late triggers penalties that are charged per member, for every month it's late. And members who work in the business generally owe self-employment tax on their share. The rules for members who don't work in the business are less settled, so that's a question for a professional.
<sub>388 chars</sub>

### 11 · The S corp election  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Election one: S corporation / A reasonable salary, / then distributions.

> Now the elections. The first is S corporation status, which we covered in detail in episode one. An owner who works in the business takes a reasonable salary through payroll, which carries payroll taxes, and the rest of the profit can come out as distributions, without self-employment tax. It's still a pass-through, so income tax works much the same. The trade-off is the cost of payroll, a separate return, and keeping the salary genuinely reasonable.

### 13 · The C corp election  `NumberCallout`
*On screen:* Election two: C corporation / 21% / Flat federal corporate income tax rate

> The second election is C corporation status. Now the business is no longer a pass-through. It pays its own federal income tax, at a flat twenty-one percent. That sounds low, but there's a catch. When the profit is paid out to the owners as dividends, it's taxed again, on their personal returns.
<sub>295 chars</sub>

### 14 · Double taxation  `KineticType`
*On screen:* $100,000 of profit, paid out as dividends / $100,000 − 21% = $79,000 / − 15% dividend tax = $67,150

> Here's how that plays out. A hundred thousand dollars of corporate profit pays twenty-one thousand in corporate tax, leaving seventy-nine thousand. Paid out as qualified dividends, at a fifteen percent rate, that's another eleven thousand eight hundred fifty. The owner keeps sixty-seven thousand one hundred fifty, and the combined tax is about thirty-three percent, before any state tax, or the extra three point eight percent that can apply at higher incomes.

### 15 · When a C corp shows up  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Less common / for owner-operators.

> That second layer is why C corp status is less common for owner-operated small businesses that pay out most of their profit. It tends to come up in more specific situations, like businesses planning to raise outside investment, or to keep most of their profit in the company to grow. It's a long-term decision, and one to model carefully with a professional.
<sub>358 chars</sub>

### 16 · Election timing  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Elections have deadlines / S corp for this year? / File by March 15.

> One more thing about elections: timing. For S corp status to apply to a whole calendar year, Form twenty-five fifty-three is generally due within two months and fifteen days of the start of that year, which works out to March fifteenth. Miss it, and the election usually starts the following year, although the IRS does offer relief for some late elections. And once you change your classification with Form eighty-eight thirty-two, you generally can't change it again for five years. These aren't decisions to make on a whim.
<sub>526 chars</sub>

### 17 · Don't forget the state  `KineticType`
*On screen:* Then there's / your state.

> And all of this is federal. States add their own layer. Many charge LLCs an annual fee or report, some impose franchise or entity-level taxes, and a few tax S corps or LLCs specifically. Two LLCs with the same federal treatment can have very different state bills, so check your state's rules.
<sub>293 chars</sub>

### 18 · Common mistakes  `BulletBuild`
*On screen:* Costly mistakes / Four to avoid / ☑ Thinking the LLC itself saves tax / ☑ Mixing business and personal / ☑ Skipping estimated payments / ☑ Treating draws as an expense

> Let's pull together the mistakes that cost owners money. Thinking that forming an LLC, by itself, saves tax. A single-member LLC taxed the default way pays the same federal tax as a sole proprietor. Mixing personal and business money, which makes your records and your deductions harder to defend. Skipping estimated payments, because nobody withholds from your profit. And treating owner draws as an expense, when they aren't deductible.
<sub>438 chars</sub>

### 19 · Questions to ask  `BulletBuild`
*On screen:* Choosing a tax treatment / Questions that decide it / ☑ Profit vs a reasonable salary? / ☑ How many owners? Who works? / ☑ Pay profit out, or keep it in? / ☑ Which state?

> So how do owners choose? A few questions do most of the work. How far is your profit above a reasonable salary for the work you do? That's the S corp question. How many owners are there, and who actually works in the business? Do you plan to pay out most of the profit, or keep it in the company? That's where C corp status comes in. And which state are you in?

### 20 · The real takeaway  `KineticType`
*On screen:* If you remember one thing / The LLC is the wrapper. / The election decides the tax.

> If you remember one thing from this video, make it this. The LLC is the legal wrapper. It's about ownership and liability. The tax treatment is a separate decision, made through a default or an election. So the useful question is never just, should I form an LLC. It's how should my LLC be taxed, and when should that change.
